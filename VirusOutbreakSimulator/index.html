    About this model

    This is a simplified educational model, not a real-world prediction tool. Its rules and numbers are invented to show general ideas about how outbreaks spread. They do not describe any real disease, city or population. Don't use it for health, policy or personal decisions.

    SEIR in one minute

    • S, Susceptible: never infected, can catch the virus.
    • E, Exposed: infected but still incubating; can't spread it yet.
    • I, Infectious: can infect others. Some never show symptoms. Others develop symptoms about a day in, and a share of those become severe and need a hospital bed.
    • R, Recovered: immune for the rest of the run (no waning immunity here).
    • D, Deceased: only severe cases can die. The risk is much higher when no hospital bed is free.

    How the city works

    Each dot is one person with a home, a household and a daily routine. Children go to school, most adults go to work, and anyone may visit shops or parks. Weekends are free time. The virus spreads when an infectious person spends time within a short distance of a susceptible one. The transmission probability is the chance of infection for each hour of that close contact. Each person's incubation and infectious periods vary randomly around the averages you set.

    What the interventions do (in this model)

    • Masks cut a wearer's chance of spreading the virus by 50% and of catching it by 30%. Adoption sets how many people wear one.
    • Vaccination protects susceptible people by the efficacy you set. The campaign vaccinates a share of the population every day.
    • Quarantine moves people with symptoms to an isolation center about half a day after their symptoms start. People with no symptoms are never caught.
    • Lockdown keeps people who comply at home. Essential workers keep working, and short grocery trips still happen.

    Major simplifications

    The model ignores real mixing patterns, ages of contacts, reinfection, testing, behaviour changing over time, travel and seasonality. It also uses a small population. Outcomes are random, so the same settings can produce very different outbreaks. Run the same scenario several times to see the range.
